Pre-decided responses

If-then rules

Decide now, while you're calm, what you'll do when the specific thing happens. In the moment, you will not be capable of good decisions — that's the whole reason this section exists.

Gollwitzer & Sheeran (2006), Advances in Experimental Social Psychology 38:69–119. Across 94 independent tests, if-then plans produced a medium-to-large improvement in goal attainment (d = 0.65) over intention alone — because the response gets triggered by the situation rather than requiring a decision.

Commitment devices

Willpower in the moment is the weakest tool available. Change what's possible instead of what's tempting.

Ariely & Wertenbroch (2002), Psychological Science 13(3):219–224. Students given the option to set their own binding deadlines chose to impose them — and performed better than students with no self-imposed constraints. People know they'll procrastinate and will pay to be stopped.
Why waiting works

Urge surfing

Smokers taught to observe cravings without acting — noticing the physical sensation, watching it crest and fall — smoked significantly less over the following week than those given standard coping advice. The instruction was not to resist. It was to watch.

Bowen & Marlatt (2009), Psychology of Addictive Behaviors 23(4):666–671.
The distinction matters. Suppression — "don't think about it" — tends to backfire. Observation doesn't, because you're not fighting anything. You're waiting.
Make the trap work for you

Temptation bundling

Only let yourself have the addictive thing while doing the thing you avoid. Gym-goers who could only listen to their favourite audiobooks at the gym raised attendance by 27–51%.

Milkman, Minson & Volpp (2014), Management Science 60(2):283–299.
